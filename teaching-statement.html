Teaching Statement

From Purpose to Understanding: Connecting Theory, Data, and Engineering Decisions

My teaching philosophy begins with purpose. Students learn abstract ideas more deeply when they understand what the knowledge makes possible: what real problem it helps solve, what decision it informs, or what phenomenon it helps explain.

Begin with the contribution a concept makes

I introduce concepts through meaningful engineering and everyday applications before asking students to master the mathematical or computational machinery. When students can see why a method matters, their curiosity, imagination, active participation, and retention increase. In probability and statistics, this means asking what a probability model represents and how variability affects an engineering conclusion. In quality engineering, it means connecting a control-chart statistic to the practical question of whether a process has meaningfully changed and what action should follow.

My experience with Advanced Quality Control reinforced this approach. Student teams investigated modern statistical monitoring methods and were expected to explain the method, reproduce its logic, and connect it to an engineering context. Projects of this kind help students trace mathematical objects to system behavior and engineering decisions.

Scaffold reasoning, then transfer ownership

I organize difficult problems into a repeatable sequence: formulate the engineering question; identify knowns, unknowns, and sources of uncertainty; select a model and state its assumptions; compute or simulate; validate the result; and interpret the answer in context. Early assignments provide prompts for these steps. Later assignments provide only the problem statement, requiring students to decide what tools, assumptions, and diagnostic checks are necessary.

I value correct results, but I also evaluate model choice, assumptions, units, diagnostic checks, interpretation, reproducibility, and communication. Larger projects can use staged deliverables such as problem formulation, preliminary analysis, code or model review, a final report, and presentation.

Use authentic problems to build transferable capability

Open-ended problems provide a bridge between classroom knowledge and the ambiguity students will face as engineers and researchers. My goal is not only mastery of course content, but development of transferable skills in problem formulation, computation, scientific communication, teamwork, critical evaluation, and independent learning.

In a future undergraduate course in Manufacturing Data Analytics, teams could build a statistical monitoring or prediction pipeline in Python using streaming engineering data and conclude with a conference-style paper and presentation. At the graduate level, a course in Physics-Informed Machine Learning and Uncertainty-Aware Digital Twins could ask students to construct, calibrate, validate, and evaluate reduced-order, neural-operator, PINN, or sparse-identification models for physical systems.

Mentoring for independence, professional development & wellbeing

My mentoring spans undergraduate, master's, recent graduate, and doctoral researchers working on 3D model reconstruction, active learning, generative CAD modeling, inverse modeling for microstructural characterization, and scientific machine learning. In two master's projects, I served as a co-mentor alongside my advisor, providing technical and research support that contributed to thesis development.

I want students' research experience to contribute directly to their professional development. Training should leave students better prepared to tackle unfamiliar real-world problems, communicate across disciplines, use computational tools responsibly, and contribute independently in their chosen fields. Because mental and emotional wellbeing affect learning and research output, I pay attention to students as people as well as researchers, help them identify appropriate resources when needed, and aim to create a climate in which asking for help is compatible with high expectations.

Inclusive access through transparent expectations & shared resources

I use transparent rubrics, worked examples, early diagnostic opportunities, and normalizing office hours and project check-ins as part of the learning process. In teams, rotating technical and communication roles distribute responsibility and provide multiple pathways for students to develop competence while preserving common technical standards.

My educational outreach reflects the same commitment to access. From October 2020 through July 2021, I volunteered as a GRE instructor, teaching quantitative reasoning, verbal reasoning, analytical writing, test-taking strategy, and structured problem solving. In 2021, EducationUSA Abuja invited me to deliver a session on GRE and TOEFL/Duolingo test-taking strategies, and I returned as a resource person in 2024. Across classroom teaching and GRE mentoring, more than 300 learners have benefited from my instruction.

Future curriculum contributions & continuous improvement

I am prepared to teach undergraduate and graduate courses in probability and statistics, quality and reliability engineering, data analytics, engineering simulation, machine learning, manufacturing systems, uncertainty quantification, and systems modeling. My research also creates opportunities for specialized instruction in scientific machine learning, inverse problems, digital twins, physics discovery, and data-driven engineering.

I treat teaching as an iterative engineering process. I use student feedback, concept-level diagnostics, rubric performance, and the quality of technical explanations to identify where students are memorizing rather than reasoning and to revise examples, scaffolds, and assessments accordingly.
