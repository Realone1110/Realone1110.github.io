Teaching Statement

From Purpose to Understanding

I fell in love with teaching as an undergraduate, when I began helping classmates work through difficult Mechanics of Machines problems by connecting equations to the motion of real mechanical systems. The satisfaction of seeing an inaccessible concept become understandable still shapes how I teach today.

My teaching is guided by three connected principles: make learning purposeful and active, make reasoning and expectations transparent, and use authentic engineering problems to develop critical thinking and independence.

1. Make learning purposeful & active

I introduce the physical or operational problem before presenting the mathematical machinery. I ask students: What is being observed? What is uncertain? What decision must be made? In probability and statistics, this moves attention from selecting a formula to interpreting variability in an engineering conclusion. In quality control, a control-chart statistic becomes evidence for deciding whether a process has changed and what action should follow.

I also make class time participatory. Questions, short problem-solving activities, interpretation of plots or process signals, and student explanations provide opportunities for active engagement and real-time formative assessment. These moments help me see where understanding is breaking down before confusion accumulates.

2. Make reasoning visible & expectations transparent

I organize difficult engineering problems around a repeatable sequence: define the question, identify what is known and unknown, recognize sources of variability or uncertainty, select a model, state assumptions, compute or simulate, check the result, and interpret it in context. Early assignments make these steps explicit; later assignments remove more scaffolding so students must decide which tools, assumptions, and diagnostic checks are needed.

I assess more than a numerical answer. Model choice, assumptions, units, diagnostics, interpretation, reproducibility, and communication all matter. In Summer 2026 IEE 570, team projects required students to explain an advanced monitoring methodology, demonstrate it through a manageable example, connect it to a real application, and discuss limitations and future research. Draft feedback gave students a chance to improve before final submission.

3. Build critical thinking through authentic engineering problems

My goal is for students to leave a course able to decide whether a method is appropriate, defend assumptions, recognize limitations, and communicate a recommendation when the problem is incomplete. Authentic projects create questions that do not appear when every quantity and method is specified in advance.

In one Summer 2026 project, a team studying Hidden Markov Models for change-point detection initially struggled with how to demonstrate the methodology and define meaningful future work. I asked them to select or simulate data, apply the method themselves, generate their own results, and consider how it could be improved. Their final project connected the method to a manufacturing maintenance problem and earned 92%. Across all ten teams, project scores ranged from 89% to 98%, with eight teams scoring at least 92%. Unsolicited student messages described the semester as “great” and my feedback and advice as “greatly beneficial.”

Mentoring experience

My mentoring philosophy extends the same progression from guided reasoning to intellectual ownership. I currently jointly mentor a four-student ASU final-year capstone team, with students from Industrial Engineering and Engineering Management, on image-based 3D reconstruction for additive-manufacturing quality assurance. I helped translate the larger research problem into capstone-scale objectives, shared literature and technical materials, established progress expectations, reviewed work, completed sponsor evaluations, and supported presentation preparation.

I also mentor a recent Mechanical Engineering graduate and a first-year Industrial Engineering Ph.D. student on the broader 3D reconstruction research program. The work combines imaging hardware, calibration, photogrammetry, photometric stereo, reconstruction, and engineering quality decisions. My goal is not simply to make a computational pipeline run, but to help students determine whether a result is physically meaningful and sufficiently validated.

Additional mentoring includes an international undergraduate researcher through ASU’s Summer Research Initiative and two master's projects, co-mentored with my advisor, in generative CAD modeling and inverse modeling for microstructural characterization. Both master's projects contributed to thesis development.

Teaching plans & continuous improvement

My background prepares me to teach undergraduate and graduate courses in probability and statistics, quality and reliability engineering, design of experiments, data analytics, engineering simulation, machine learning, manufacturing systems, and uncertainty quantification. My capstone mentoring also prepares me to contribute to senior design and project-based engineering courses.

UNDERGRADUATE

Manufacturing Data Analytics

Students would work with vibration, temperature, image, or other process data to connect statistical reasoning, computation, and engineering decisions.

GRADUATE

Physics-Based Machine Learning for Engineers

Students would build and validate surrogate, operator-learning, or physics-discovery models for physical systems while examining assumptions, uncertainty, and model trustworthiness.

I view teaching itself as an iterative engineering process. Student questions, participation, project quality, concept-level performance, and structured feedback provide evidence about where examples, pacing, scaffolding, or assessments should change. Ultimately, I want students to explain not only how an answer was obtained, but why it is reasonable, what assumptions support it, what uncertainty remains, and what engineering decision it enables.
