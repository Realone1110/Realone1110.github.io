Teaching & Mentoring

Connecting theory, data, and engineering decisions

My teaching philosophy grew from an experience I had as an undergraduate, when I began helping classmates work through difficult Mechanics of Machines problems by connecting equations to the motion of real mechanical systems. Seeing an inaccessible concept become understandable made teaching a lasting part of the academic career I wanted.

I now organize my teaching around three principles: make learning purposeful and active, make reasoning and expectations transparent, and use authentic engineering problems to develop critical thinking and independence.

Teaching principles

PURPOSE & PARTICIPATION

Make learning purposeful and active

I introduce the physical or operational problem before the mathematical machinery and ask what is observed, what is uncertain, and what decision must be made. Questions, short activities, plot interpretation, and student explanations keep class time participatory while giving me real-time evidence of where understanding is breaking down.

REASONING

Make the problem-solving process visible

I help students move through formulation, knowns and unknowns, sources of variability, model choice, assumptions, computation, validation, and interpretation. As confidence grows, I remove scaffolding so students decide for themselves which tools and diagnostic checks are appropriate.

AUTHENTIC PROBLEMS

Build critical thinking and independence

I use open-ended engineering problems that require students to defend assumptions, recognize limitations, and communicate recommendations when the problem is incomplete. The goal is not merely to reproduce a familiar method, but to decide whether a method is appropriate and what its result means.

Evidence from project-based learning

In Summer 2026 IEE 570, each team was asked to explain an advanced monitoring methodology, demonstrate it through a manageable example, connect it to a real application, and discuss limitations and future research. I invited students to share drafts and provided specific feedback they could use before final submission.

One team studying Hidden Markov Models for change-point detection initially struggled with how to demonstrate the methodology. Rather than give them a completed solution, I asked them to select or simulate data, apply the method themselves, generate their own results, and consider how it could be improved. Their final work connected the method to a manufacturing maintenance problem and earned 92%. Across all ten teams, project scores ranged from 89% to 98%, with eight teams scoring at least 92%. Unsolicited student messages described the semester as “great” and my feedback and advice as “greatly beneficial.”

Research mentoring

My mentoring extends the same progression from guided reasoning to intellectual ownership. I use clear short-term goals, regular research discussions, reproducible workflows, and iterative feedback to help students move from implementing assigned tasks toward making and defending methodological choices.

Mentoring philosophy

STRUCTURE

Make expectations explicit

I begin with clear objectives, intermediate milestones, reproducible workflows, and regular feedback so students understand both the technical goal and a practical path toward independent work.

OWNERSHIP

Build independent judgment

I gradually shift responsibility for literature synthesis, model selection, experiment design, validation, interpretation, and communication to the student.

TRANSFER

Develop skills beyond one project

I emphasize transferable skills in problem formulation, computational practice, technical communication, collaboration, and decision making so research training contributes to long-term professional development.

WELLBEING

Support the whole learner

I aim to maintain a respectful environment where students can communicate challenges, receive appropriate support, and be directed to relevant resources without treating help-seeking as incompatible with high expectations.

FEEDBACK

Use feedback for shared growth

I value timely, specific, constructive feedback and also treat student responses and outcomes as evidence for improving my own teaching and mentoring.

COMMUNICATION

Teach the reasoning, not only the result

I ask students to explain assumptions, defend methodological choices, diagnose failures, and communicate what a result means physically or operationally.
